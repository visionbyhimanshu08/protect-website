Builder Quotes

6 questions

Not necessarily. A fixed-price contract can still move through provisional sums, prime cost items, site costs and variations. We check exactly which parts of your quote are genuinely fixed and which are allowances that can grow.

A provisional sum is an estimate for work the builder cannot price accurately yet, such as excavation or retaining walls. If the real cost is higher, you pay the difference plus the builder’s margin — so low allowances can make a quote look cheaper than it really is.

A prime cost (PC) item is an allowance for items you have not yet chosen, like tapware, appliances or tiles. If your selections cost more than the allowance, you pay the difference. We check that PC allowances are realistic for the finish level you expect.

Site costs cover the work needed to prepare your block — soil conditions, slope, fill, rock, drainage and service connections. They are often quoted before a full soil test and survey, which is why they are one of the most common reasons a budget blows out.

Line them up item by item: inclusions, exclusions, allowances, site costs, specifications and contract terms. The cheaper quote is often missing items the other one includes. Our Quote Review does this comparison for you and shows the real difference.

Slow down. Deadlines like “price rise next week” are a common sales tactic. A reputable builder will give you time to have the quote and contract reviewed independently before you commit — and that review is worth far more than any discount.

Building Contracts

5 questions

A lawyer can advise on the legal terms, but many problems in building contracts are construction issues — vague specifications, unrealistic allowances and missing inclusions. We review the contract from a builder’s point of view and can work alongside your lawyer if you have one.

The HIA contract is a standard form published by the Housing Industry Association, an industry body that represents builders. It is widely used and compliant, but it is written from the builder’s perspective, so it is important to understand the clauses that affect you and any special conditions added.

It is the period after handover during which the builder must return to fix defects that appear in the work. This is separate from the statutory warranties that protect you for longer. We explain how both apply to your contract and what to do before the period ends.

Yes. Special conditions, payment stages, allowances, inclusions and timeframes can all be discussed before you sign. We help you identify which points are worth negotiating and how to raise them with your builder.

Most contracts allow for extensions of time for reasons such as weather or variations, and include liquidated damages if the builder is late without a valid reason. We check whether delays are justified and help you respond in writing.

Variations

4 questions

Not automatically. A variation should be in writing, explain why it is needed and state its cost and effect on time. We check whether the variation is valid, whether the work was already included in your contract, and whether the price is fair.

In most cases a builder should not proceed with a variation until you have agreed to it in writing, with limited exceptions such as urgent work. If work has gone ahead without your approval, talk to us before you pay for it.

A few variations are normal. A steady stream of them usually points to a vague contract, low allowances or a builder recovering margin. We review the pattern as well as each item and help you push back where it is warranted.

A variation is a change to the agreed work. A defect is work that does not meet the contract, the plans or the required standards. You should never be charged a variation to fix a defect — that is the builder’s cost.

Mandatory inspections by the building surveyor check compliance at key points, not workmanship or whether the build matches your contract. An independent inspection works for you and looks for the defects and shortcuts that compliance checks are not designed to catch.

Builder Problems and Disputes

5 questions

Write to them — email creates a record. Check the site, your contract and your payment history, and avoid paying ahead of progress. If silence continues, contact us early; the sooner a problem is addressed, the more options you have.

Warning signs include unexplained delays, trades leaving site, requests for early payments, unpaid supplier complaints and frequent excuses. One sign on its own is not proof, but several together are worth acting on.

DBI protects homeowners if their builder dies, disappears or becomes insolvent and cannot complete the work or fix defects. In Victoria it is generally required for domestic building work above a set value. Your builder should give you a certificate before taking a deposit — check you have it.

Contact the liquidator and your DBI insurer as soon as possible, secure the site and gather your documents. We help you assess the work completed, prepare the information your insurer needs and plan how to finish the build.

VCAT is the Victorian Civil and Administrative Tribunal. Most domestic building disputes must first go through Domestic Building Dispute Resolution Victoria before they can be heard at VCAT. We help you prepare clear evidence of the issues at each step.

Costs and Budgeting

4 questions

It depends heavily on design, size, finishes, block conditions and the builder. Rather than relying on a price per square metre, we help you build a realistic budget that includes site costs, upgrades, fees and contingency.

Common exclusions include landscaping, driveways, fencing, window coverings, some site costs, authority fees and connections, and upgrades from display homes. We list what is missing so there are no surprises later.

We generally recommend setting aside a contingency on top of the contract price, and more for sloping or difficult blocks. The right amount depends on how many allowances and unknowns remain in your contract.

The usual causes are provisional sums that ran over, PC items upgraded, site costs and a series of variations. We can review where the extra cost came from and whether each item was properly agreed.

Planning and Land

3 questions

Look at slope, soil, easements, overlays, covenants, service connections and access. These factors drive site costs and what you can build. We review the block with you before you commit so the budget is not decided by surprises.

A Section 32 is the vendor’s statement that comes with land in Victoria. It discloses easements, covenants, planning controls and services — all of which affect what and how you can build. We read it from a building point of view, alongside your conveyancer.

A soil test classifies the ground your slab will sit on. Reactive or unstable soil can add significant cost to the foundations. Where possible, get one done or review an existing report before you buy.
